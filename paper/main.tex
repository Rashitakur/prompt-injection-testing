\begin{abstract}
Applications that pass untrusted text to large language models (LLMs) need a
first-line filter for prompt injections that runs on every input, adds little latency,
and rarely blocks legitimate users. We compare five families of detectors under one
protocol: a TF-IDF baseline, logistic-regression probes on frozen sentence embeddings,
a fine-tuned DistilBERT classifier, perplexity-based filters, and LLM-as-judge detectors
with 1.5B--8B parameters. Every detector is calibrated with the same validation-based
threshold rule and evaluated on an in-distribution test set, on held-out data sources,
on benign inputs designed to provoke over-defense, and on synthetic adversarial-suffix
and noisy-benign stress sets. Latency is measured end to end on the same GPU and CPU.
\claim{The fine-tuned 66M-parameter DistilBERT reaches an in-distribution F1 of
\res{distilbert}{test-id}{f1} at a median latency of
\res{distilbert}{lat}{cuda-bs1-p50}\,ms per request on GPU, while the strongest LLM judge
reaches \res{judge-qwen7b}{test-id}{f1} at \res{judge-qwen7b}{lat}{cuda-bs1-p50}\,ms.
Perplexity filters are close to chance on natural-language injections
(AUROC \res{ppl-gpt2-ppl}{test-id}{auroc}) and flag benign inputs that contain
high-entropy content such as code, URLs or base64, but they detect gibberish adversarial
suffixes.}
\claim{All supervised detectors lose accuracy on held-out sources and over-flag benign
prompts that contain trigger words.} A cascade that escalates only the
\res{cascade}{test-id}{q10-esc-pct}\% least confident inputs from DistilBERT to the
LLM judge reaches an F1 of \res{cascade}{test-id}{q10-f1} at an expected
\res{cascade}{test-id}{q10-ms}\,ms per request. We release the full pipeline,
including SLURM job files, so that every number in this paper can be regenerated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM-integrated applications pass text from users, web pages, e-mails, documents and tool
outputs into a model that also receives the developer's instructions. Because the
model cannot reliably separate instructions from data, an attacker who controls any of
that text can try to override the intended behaviour. This is a \emph{prompt
injection}~\citep{perez2022ignore,greshake2023not,liu2023houyi,liu2024formalizing}.
The attacker may want the model to ignore its task, leak its system prompt or private
data, or call tools on the attacker's behalf. Prevention-oriented defenses change how
models are trained or prompted~\citep{chen2024struq,wallace2024instruction,hines2024spotlighting}.
Detection-oriented defenses instead screen inputs before they reach the model, and they
are often the only option for teams that deploy a third-party model through an API.

A deployed detector must meet three requirements at once, and they pull against each
other:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy and generalisation.} It must catch injections written in styles
        and domains that were not in its training data.
  \item \textbf{Few false positives.} A detector that blocks benign prompts mentioning
        ``ignore'', ``instructions'' or ``system'' degrades the product. This failure mode
        is known as \emph{over-defense}~\citep{li2024injecguard}.
  \item \textbf{Low latency and cost.} The filter runs on every request, and in
        retrieval-augmented or agentic settings on every retrieved chunk and tool output,
        so its cost is multiplied many times per user interaction.
\end{enumerate}
Published detectors are usually evaluated on only one of these axes, with different
datasets, thresholds and hardware, so practitioners cannot read off the trade-off
between them. This paper measures all three for one set of detectors under one
protocol. We ask:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How accurate are lightweight detectors (TF-IDF, embedding probes, a fine-tuned
             small encoder) compared with perplexity filters and LLM judges on
             in-distribution data?
  \item[RQ2] How well does each detector family generalise to data sources held out from training?
  \item[RQ3] How often does each family raise false alarms on benign inputs that contain
             injection-like trigger words or high-entropy content?
  \item[RQ4] What does each family cost in latency on a GPU and on a CPU, and can a cascade
             combine the cost of a small model with the robustness of a large one?
\end{description}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A reproducible benchmark harness that removes duplicates and train--test leakage,
holds out entire data sources, and includes over-defense and stress sets;
(ii) a comparison of five detector families in which every method gets the same
validation-based threshold calibration;
(iii) end-to-end latency measurements at batch size~1 and~32 on a shared GPU and CPU;
(iv) an analysis of a cheap-then-expensive cascade; and
(v) an open-source implementation with SLURM job files that regenerates every table and
figure in this paper.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Prompt-injection attacks.}
\citet{perez2022ignore} showed that handcrafted inputs can make a model ignore its prompt
(goal hijacking) or leak it (prompt leaking). \citet{greshake2023not} introduced
\emph{indirect} prompt injection, where the attack sits in content the application
retrieves rather than in the user's message. \citet{liu2023houyi} attacked deployed
LLM-integrated applications, and \citet{liu2024formalizing} formalised injection attacks
and benchmarked defenses against them. Large crowd-sourced attack collections come
from online games and competitions such as Gandalf, Tensor Trust~\citep{toyer2023tensor}
and HackAPrompt~\citep{schulhoff2023hackaprompt}. Jailbreaks~\citep{wei2023jailbroken}
target the model's safety training rather than the application's instructions. The two
overlap in practice, and the public datasets we use mix them.

\paragraph{Detection-based defenses.}
\citet{jain2023baseline} proposed perplexity and windowed-perplexity filters as baseline
defenses against optimisation-based attacks such as GCG~\citep{zou2023universal}, whose
adversarial suffixes have very high perplexity. \citet{alon2023detecting} combined
perplexity with token length in a classifier. Classifier guardrails fine-tune small
encoders for the task. Examples include Meta's Prompt Guard, the ProtectAI DeBERTa-v3
model~\citep{protectai2024deberta} and InjecGuard~\citep{li2024injecguard}. InjecGuard
also showed that such guardrails over-flag benign text containing trigger words and
released the NotInject benchmark to measure this. LLM-based moderation such as Llama
Guard~\citep{inan2023llamaguard} and the broader LLM-as-a-judge
paradigm~\citep{zheng2023judging} trade much higher cost for flexibility and
zero-shot use. \citet{liu2024formalizing} evaluated known-answer detection, and more
recent work reads signals from the protected model's internals, such as activation-based
task drift~\citep{abdelnabi2024track} and attention patterns~\citep{hung2024attention}.
BIPIA~\citep{yi2023benchmarking} benchmarks indirect injection.

\paragraph{Gap.}
We know of no study that compares these detector families on accuracy, out-of-source
generalisation, over-defense \emph{and} measured latency under a shared calibration
protocol and shared hardware. Without that, it is not clear whether an LLM judge's extra
cost buys extra robustness. This work provides that comparison.

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

A detector $f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ maps a text segment $x$ to a score; higher means
more likely to be an injection. $x$ may be a user prompt or a piece of retrieved content.
A deployment flags $x$ when $f(x)\ge\tau$. The detector sees only $x$; it does not see the
protected application's system prompt or the model's internals. This is the common
``input-filter'' deployment, and it lets a single detector serve many applications. We
consider attackers who write natural-language injections, as in the public datasets. We
also include a synthetic stress test with gibberish adversarial suffixes, standing in
for optimisation-based attacks (\S\ref{sec:data}). We do not consider adaptive attacks
optimised against a specific detector, and we discuss this limitation in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}

All detectors output a continuous score. Thresholds are chosen outside the detector with
one shared rule (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\paragraph{TF-IDF + LR.} Character 2--5-gram and word 1--2-gram TF-IDF features feed an
L2-regularised logistic regression with balanced class weights. $C$ is selected on
validation AUROC. This is the cheapest baseline and needs no GPU.

\paragraph{Embedding + LR.} A frozen sentence encoder produces an $\ell_2$-normalised
embedding, and a logistic-regression probe is trained on top. We use all-MiniLM-L6-v2
(22M parameters, mean pooling)~\citep{wang2020minilm,reimers2019sentence} and
BGE-small-en-v1.5 (33M, CLS pooling). The encoder can be shared with an application's
retrieval stack, so the probe's marginal cost can be close to zero.

\paragraph{Fine-tuned DistilBERT.} DistilBERT-base-uncased~\citep{sanh2019distilbert}
(66M parameters) is fine-tuned end to end with cross-entropy, AdamW (learning rate
$3\times10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.01, 10\% linear warm-up), batch size 32, maximum length
256 tokens and 3 epochs. The best epoch is selected by validation AUROC. We train with
three seeds and report mean~$\pm$~standard deviation.

\paragraph{Perplexity filters.} A small causal LM (GPT-2, 124M~\citep{radford2019language})
assigns each token $x_t$ a negative log-likelihood $\ell_t=-\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$ (a BOS
token is prepended so that every token is scored). We derive three detectors from one
forward pass:
\begin{align}
  f_{\textsc{ppl}}(x) &= \tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t, \qquad
  f_{\textsc{win}}(x) = \max_{s}\tfrac{1}{w}\textstyle\sum_{t=s}^{s+w-1}\ell_t \quad (w=16),\\
  f_{\textsc{lr}}(x) &= \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^\top[\,f_{\textsc{ppl}},\,f_{\textsc{win}},\,\mathrm{std}(\ell),\,\log T\,]+b\right).
\end{align}
$f_{\textsc{ppl}}$ and $f_{\textsc{win}}$ are the unsupervised filters of
\citet{jain2023baseline}. $f_{\textsc{lr}}$ is a supervised variant in the spirit of
\citet{alon2023detecting}.

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge.} An instruction-tuned LLM gets a system prompt and a definition
of prompt injection that explicitly excludes benign discussion of security topics
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). It is asked to answer ``Yes'' or ``No''. Instead of sampling
an answer, we read the next-token distribution once and use
\begin{equation}
  f_{\textsc{judge}}(x)=\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y\mid\text{prompt}(x))}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y\mid\text{prompt}(x))+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}p(n\mid\text{prompt}(x))},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Y}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ are the first sub-word tokens of the casing variants
of ``Yes'' and ``No''. This needs a single forward pass with no decoding, and it gives a
continuous score for threshold-free metrics. Each judge is also reported at its natural
threshold of $0.5$. We evaluate Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\citep{qwen2025qwen25}.
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct can be enabled in the released configuration.

\paragraph{Reference detector.} We also run the public ProtectAI
DeBERTa-v3-base injection classifier~\citep{protectai2024deberta} zero-shot. It may have
been trained on some of the same public data, so its numbers are marked with
$\dagger$ and are \emph{not} a clean held-out comparison.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Table~\ref{tab:data} lists all splits.

\textbf{In-distribution (ID).} Training, validation and ID test data come from
\texttt{deepset/prompt-injections} and \texttt{xTRam1/safe-guard-prompt-injection}. Each
source's own test split forms the ID test set. The validation set is a 15\% split of the
training pool, stratified by source and label, and it is used only for model selection and
threshold calibration.

\textbf{Held-out sources (OOD).} These sources are never seen in training:
(i) the user prompts of SPML~\citep{sharma2024spml}, which contain injections against
chatbot system prompts;
(ii) \texttt{jackhhao/jailbreak-classification}, which pairs jailbreak prompts with benign prompts;
(iii) Gandalf~\citep{lakera2023gandalf} ``ignore instructions'' attacks paired with an
equal-scale sample of benign Dolly-15k~\citep{conover2023dolly} instructions.

\textbf{Over-defense.} NotInject~\citep{li2024injecguard} contains benign sentences that
are deliberately packed with trigger words. Every flag on it is a false positive.

\textbf{Synthetic stress sets.} \emph{Adv.\ suffix} appends a 20-token random-character
suffix, imitating optimisation-based attacks~\citep{zou2023universal}, to ID test
injections and pairs them with untouched benign ID test prompts. \emph{Noisy benign}
appends benign high-entropy content (base64, hex digests, URLs with tracking parameters,
code snippets, UUIDs) to benign ID test prompts. It measures how often a detector mistakes
unusual-but-harmless text for an attack.

\textbf{Hygiene.} Texts are truncated to 4{,}000 characters. Exact duplicates
(after whitespace and case normalisation) are removed. Duplicates with conflicting labels
are dropped entirely, and any evaluation text that also occurs in the training data is
removed. This step is important because public injection datasets overlap heavily. The
numbers of removed rows are logged in \texttt{manifest.json}.

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textbf{Shared calibration.} Each detector's threshold $\tau$ is the value that maximises
F1 on the validation set. This includes zero-shot methods, so that differences between
methods are not just differences in thresholds. We report F1, AUROC, AUPRC, the false
positive rate (FPR) at $\tau$, and the TPR at 1\% FPR. On benign-only sets we report the
FPR, and on positive-only sources the recall. For multi-seed models we report
mean~$\pm$~std over three seeds. 95\% confidence intervals use a 1{,}000-sample stratified
percentile bootstrap, and pairwise differences use a paired bootstrap test
(Appendix~\ref{app:sig}).

\textbf{Latency.} For each detector we load its fitted state and score 300 ID test texts
sampled with a fixed seed. Timing is end to end, including tokenisation and feature
extraction. We take 10 warm-up calls and synchronise CUDA before reading the clock.
Batch size~1 gives per-request latency (we report the median, p50). Batch size~32 gives
throughput. All GPU timings use one \gpuname\ on an exclusively allocated node. CPU
timings use \cpuname\ with 8 threads. The 7B/8B judges are not timed on CPU.

\textbf{Cascade.} The cheap detector (DistilBERT) decides every input whose score is far
from its threshold. The fraction $q$ of inputs closest to the threshold, set on
validation, is escalated to the LLM judge. The expected latency is
$\mathbb{E}[\text{ms}]=\text{ms}_\text{cheap}+\hat{q}\cdot\text{ms}_\text{judge}$, where
$\hat q$ is the fraction actually escalated on the test set.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Practical recommendations.}
\claim{(1) A fine-tuned small encoder is the right default first-line filter. It is the
most accurate in-distribution, runs comfortably on CPU, and its threshold can be tuned
for a target FPR. (2) Its training data must include hard benign negatives, such as
NotInject-style prompts, or it will over-block. (3) Spend LLM-judge budget through a
cascade rather than on every request. (4) Use perplexity only as a narrow complementary
signal against optimisation-based gibberish attacks, and never on retrieved content that
legitimately contains code or encoded data. (5) Calibrate every detector, zero-shot ones
included, on in-domain validation data before deployment.}

\paragraph{Why do the lightweight classifiers generalise worse?}
\claim{Their decision rules rest heavily on surface cues, as the closeness of TF-IDF and
DistilBERT in-distribution suggests. Public injection datasets share a narrow set of
templates (``ignore the above'', ``you are now DAN''). Such cues transfer to sources that
reuse the templates (Gandalf) but not to stylistically different attacks (long
jailbreak role-plays) or to benign text that happens to contain the cues (NotInject).}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-adaptive attacker.} We do not optimise attacks against each detector.
        Small classifiers are known to be vulnerable to such attacks, and an adaptive
        evaluation would likely lower every number reported here.
  \item \textbf{Labels and scope.} Public datasets mix prompt injection and jailbreaking,
        contain label noise, and are almost entirely English. We evaluate single text
        segments, not full indirect-injection pipelines such as BIPIA~\citep{yi2023benchmarking}.
  \item \textbf{Judge design.} Judge accuracy depends on the prompt. We use one fixed,
        reasonable prompt and do not tune it, which favours neither the judges nor the
        other detectors but leaves possible gains unexplored.
  \item \textbf{Reference contamination.} The ProtectAI reference may have seen some of our
        evaluation sources during its training.
  \item \textbf{Hardware dependence.} Absolute latencies depend on hardware and software
        stack. The relative ordering is more stable than the absolute numbers.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ethics.}
Detection research could in principle help attackers probe filters. All data are public,
we release no new attacks, and the synthetic stress tests use random strings rather
than optimised adversarial suffixes.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\claim{Lightweight prompt-injection detectors are accurate and fast in-distribution but
brittle under source shift and prone to over-defense. LLM judges are more robust but
costly. Perplexity filters solve a different and narrower problem.} A
confidence-based cascade recovers much of the judge's accuracy at a small fraction of its
cost. The released pipeline makes it simple to add new detectors and datasets and to
re-run the full comparison on a SLURM cluster.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Code, configuration and job files: \url{https://github.com/ANONYMOUS/pi-detect}. Running
\texttt{bash slurm/submit\_all.sh} regenerates every table and figure in this paper.
